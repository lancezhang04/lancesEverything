# Isabel — Block One

A 3×/week full-body training block for a returning multi-sport athlete.
Three distinct full-body sessions inside a 45-minute cap, built to survive an erratic college schedule
and an IT band that occasionally complains.

*Design document — program, coaching notes, and evidence base.*

---

## 1. Athlete profile

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Training age | Returning athlete. Multi-sport in HS (field hockey, soccer, equestrian), comfortable with gym equipment, detrained ~1 yr (first year of college) |
| Goals | Stronger / more athletic; wants to lose weight gained over the past year |
| Availability | 3×/week, **45 min hard cap**, erratic scheduling — no reliable day-of-week rotation |
| Other load | IM soccer ~1×/week, recreational, no practices |
| Injury | Occasional IT band pain, prior history. Not severe, but the main design constraint |
| Facility | Campus rec center — full equipment, assume crowded at peak hours |
| Coaching | Mostly solo, remote check-ins → the program must be self-explanatory |
| Nutrition | **Not currently being addressed** |

## 2. The program in one sentence

Three full-body sessions a week — same skeleton, varied movements — moderate reps taken close
enough to failure to matter — built so that missing a session costs nothing, and
with hip abductor work on the single-leg day, because that's what keeps her IT band quiet.

### The five rules

1. **A/B/C is a queue, not a calendar.** Whenever she trains, she does the next session
   in sequence. Never "Monday is A." If she misses a week, she picks up where she left off.
2. **Every session is complete on its own.** All three carry the same five slots — only the
   main lower lift and its accessory rotate. A missed session costs nothing structurally.
3. **8–12 reps everywhere.** One range to remember. The main lift gets 3 sets and full rest;
   everything else gets 2 sets and short rest.
4. **2–3 reps in reserve for weeks 1–2, then 1–2.** Technique consolidates first.
5. **Double progression.** All sets at 12 reps with effort in hand → add load next time.
   Otherwise keep the load and add a rep. That's the entire system.

> **The organizing principle: repeat where skill is the limiter, vary where it isn't.**
> The three main lower lifts are genuinely skill-demanding, so the pool stays small and they
> recur across the week — leg press and RDL twice each, the split squat once. That's the fastest
> route to competence, and it's also what gives double progression enough resolution to fire:
> two exposures a week means two chances to hit the top of the range. The upper slots are low-skill, so
> varying them is nearly free, and it's what keeps three sessions from feeling like one session
> on repeat. Simplicity lives in the structure (same five slots, same rep range, same rules),
> not in the movement count.
>
> **Calibration fallback:** in weeks 1–2, if any variant feels like too much to juggle,
> doing session A's version of that slot is always acceptable.

## 3. The movements

### The lower body repeats — skill lives here

| Movement | Pattern | Appears | Why this one |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leg press | Squat | A (main), B (accessory) | Loads heavy with no spotter and no rack queue. Upgrade to hack or barbell squat once form is confirmed. |
| Dumbbell Romanian deadlift | Hinge | B (main), A (accessory) | Self-limiting, far lower skill than a trap bar pull. |
| Dumbbell split squat | Single leg | C (main) | Rear foot on the floor, not elevated. Frontal-plane control without a balance circus. |
| Seated leg curl | Knee flexion | C (accessory) | The only knee flexion in the program. The RDL is hip extension, so the short head of biceps femoris would otherwise go untrained — relevant to a returning soccer player. Replaced the 45° back extension, which was a third helping of hip extension. |

### The upper body varies — these are low-skill, so variety is nearly free

| Movement | Pattern | Appears | Why this one |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chest press, flat | Horizontal push | A | Pick machine or DB in week 1 and keep it. |
| Incline DB press | Incline push | B | Upper chest and a different shoulder angle. |
| DB overhead press | Vertical push | C | Anterior delt. Pairs with the lateral raise in A for delt coverage — not variety. |
| Lat pulldown | Vertical pull | A | Always available, easy to load in small jumps. |
| Seated cable row | Horizontal pull | B, C | Rhomboids and mid-back, which a pulldown-only program misses. |

### Slot 5 — one movement, two working sets

| Movement | Appears | Why this one |
|---|---|---|
| **Hip abduction machine** | C | The IT band work. Loads on a stack, best glute med–to–TFL ratio. |
| Standing calf raise | B | Coverage, not variety — the one muscle group nothing else in the block trained. |
| DB lateral raise | A | Lateral delt was the one group under the 4-set MED, and it does the most visible work on a KPI that deliberately isn't scale weight. |

Slot 5 used to be **two** movements, run inside slots 2 and 3's rest periods. It is now one
movement with two working sets of its own, because the rest-period version wasn't getting done.
It asked her to abandon a machine mid-rest, cross the floor, do a set of something else, and be
back ninety seconds later — at a campus rec, at peak hour, which §1 lists as a design constraint.
Four minutes buys a slot that actually happens.

> ⚠️ **What that costs, and it isn't small.** Collapsing two movements into one removes the
> block's **trunk work entirely** — side plank and dead bug are gone, six weekly sets to zero —
> and leaves **abduction on session C alone**, two loaded sets a week where it had four. §14 ●07
> is the highest-confidence claim in this document and it argues for more of that work, not
> less, and §11 had side planks under *Emphasize*.
>
> The defence is narrow, and worth stating plainly rather than dressing up: two progressible
> sets that happen beat six that don't. But this is a floor, not a new baseline. If the IT band
> starts talking, §10's first lever is to put abduction into slot 5 on all three days and give
> up the calf and delt work to do it.

### Swaps — when the machine is taken

A campus rec at peak hour is a stated design constraint (§1), so every movement carries a short
list of substitutes, surfaced in the app beside its cue. Names only, in rough order of preference.
A swap is for one session, not a program change — the next session goes back to the prescription.

| Movement | Swaps |
|---|---|
| Leg press | Hack squat · Goblet squat · Barbell back squat |
| DB Romanian deadlift | Barbell RDL · Single-leg RDL · Seated leg curl |
| DB split squat | Rear-foot-elevated split squat · Walking lunge · Step-up |
| Seated leg curl | Lying leg curl · Standing single-leg curl · 45° back extension |
| Chest press | DB bench press · Barbell bench press · Push-up |
| Incline DB press | Incline machine press · Incline barbell press · Incline cable press |
| DB overhead press | Machine shoulder press · Seated barbell press · Landmine press |
| Lat pulldown | Assisted pull-up · Neutral-grip pulldown · Single-arm pulldown |
| DB lateral raise | Cable lateral raise · Machine lateral raise · Plate front-and-out raise |
| Seated cable row | Chest-supported row · Single-arm DB row · Machine row |

| Standing calf raise | Seated calf raise · Leg press calf press · Single-leg calf raise |

> Note what this quietly restores: **side-lying abduction and the banded lateral walk are back**,
> as fallbacks for a busy abduction machine rather than as programmed work. That's the right home
> for them — §14.08's objection was that a band can't be progressively loaded, which matters for
> the thing doing the work and not at all for a one-session substitute.

## 4. Session template

| # | Slot | Sets | Reps | Rest | Rotates? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Main lower lift** (protected) | 3 | 8–12 | 2–3 min | Yes — squat / hinge / single leg |
| 2 | Upper push | 2 | 8–12 | 60–90 s | Yes — flat / incline / overhead |
| 3 | Upper pull | 2 | 8–12 | 60–90 s | Yes — pulldown / row |
| 4 | Lower accessory | 2 | 8–12 | 60–90 s | Yes — whatever slot 1 isn't |
| 5 | Isolation | 2 | 12–15 | 90 s | Yes — delts / calves / abduction |

**Slot 1 is the only lift that gets full rest** — 2–3 minutes between working sets. Everything
after it rests a flat 90 seconds, and every slot opens with a one-minute setup step for finding
the machine and loading it.

> **"Accessory" means 2 sets, short rest, not protected — it does not mean easy.** B's leg press
> is still a hard set by design. Tell her that, or a brutal second set reads as her having gone
> too heavy. It self-caps without any instruction to go lighter: 60–90 seconds of rest, fourth in
> the session, after three sets of RDL, she'll settle well below her A-day load on her own.

> ⚠️ **Ordering detail that matters:** slot 5 comes *last*, and on session C that is the whole
> point. Fatiguing the hip abductors before split squats would degrade exactly the frontal-plane
> control this program exists to protect — so the abduction work has to sit after slot 1, never
> before or between its sets.

## 5. What rotates

The lower-body pair in A and B swaps emphasis; everything else is a genuinely different session.

| # | **A** — squat | **B** — hinge | **C** — single leg |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Leg press** · 3×8–12 | **DB Romanian deadlift** · 3×8–12 | **DB split squat** · 3×8–12 ea |
| **2** | Chest press (flat) · 2×8–12 | Incline DB press · 2×8–12 | DB overhead press · 2×8–12 |
| **3** | Lat pulldown · 2×8–12 | Seated cable row · 2×8–12 | Seated cable row · 2×8–12 |
| **4** | DB Romanian deadlift · 2×8–12 | Leg press · 2×8–12 | Seated leg curl · 2×8–12 |
| **5** | DB lateral raise · 2×12–15 | Standing calf raise · 2×12–15 | Hip abduction machine · 2×12–15 |

Every day carries one knee-dominant push and one hip-dominant pull — that's what slot 4 is for,
and it's why slot 4 is "whatever slot 1 isn't."

### Two slots that aren't obvious

| Slot | Instead of | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **C4** seated leg curl | 45° back extension | The RDL already supplies 5 weekly sets of hip extension; a back extension made it a third helping and left knee flexion at zero. The leg curl adds the missing function and is still a hip/pull complement to the split squat, so C's push/pull pairing holds. Seated, not lying — hip flexed puts the hamstring at longer length. |
| **A5** DB lateral raise | Hip abduction | Lateral delt was the only muscle group under the 4-set MED, at two. A raise is zero-skill and is the group doing the most visible work on a KPI that deliberately isn't scale weight. Abduction survives on C, which is the day that needs it most. |

⚠️ **The cost, and it compounded.** Slot 5 started at 6 abduction + 4 side plank. The calf raise
took it to 4 + 4, the lateral raise to 4 + 2, and moving the slot out of the rest periods — one
movement a day instead of two — to **2 + 0**. Each step was defensible alone; together they took
the lateral-chain work that §2 calls the non-negotiable down by two thirds, and the trunk work to
nothing.

What survives is the half §14 ●07 actually rests on: loaded, progressible abduction on a stack,
now on session C only. That is the floor. Nothing else comes out of slot 5, and if the IT band
starts talking, §10's lever is to put abduction back on all three days.

**Dropping the back extension** also leaves no *dynamic* erector work. Acceptable: 5 weekly sets
of RDL and 4 of seated row are both substantial isometric erector loading.

### Warm-up — identical every session

**5 min low-impact cardio** (rower, elliptical, or incline walk). That's it.

No banded lateral walk, and no separate movement prep. Slot 1 already opens with ramp-up sets,
and those are better prep than a general primer because they rehearse the exact lift she's about
to load. The band walk was carrying a second job — standing, weight-bearing abduction closer to
where frontal-plane control actually fails — but it was doing it with four discrete tensions and
no way to log progress, which is the same argument §14.08 makes for preferring the machine.

**Not running.** The repeated impact is the classic IT band aggravator, which makes it the one
modality this warm-up rules out rather than merely discourages.

## 6. Time budget

| Segment | Minutes | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cardio warm-up | 5 | Low-impact modalities only; never running |
| Slot 1 — main lift | 14.5 | Setup, two ramp-ups, three working sets, full rests |
| Slot 2 — upper push | 5 | Setup, two sets, one 90 s rest |
| Slot 3 — upper pull | 5 | |
| Slot 4 — lower accessory | 5 | |
| Slot 5 — isolation | 5 | |
| **Total** | **~39.5** | Around 5 minutes of headroom |

Transitions no longer get a line of their own. Every slot opens with a one-minute setup step, so
the walk to the next machine is inside the budget rather than beside it — which is also the only
honest place for it, since it's the part that reliably runs long.

**Session C lands at ~39** on the same arithmetic: its slot 1 trades two ramp-ups for one and
buys longer working sets with the difference, because 3 sets of split squats is really 6. If it
runs over, drop slot 1 to **2 working sets** — the same lever §10 uses after a soccer game. Don't
cut slot 4: it carries the program's only knee flexion. (This is also why C's row is the
two-handed seated row, not a single-arm version — one per-limb movement per session.)

> **Why deliberately under budget:** finishing early is the correct error. A session she
> finishes in 38 minutes feeling strong beats one she abandons at 55.

## 7. Weeks 1–2 — calibration, not training

### The frame

> *"The next two weeks aren't really training — they're calibration. We're finding out where
> you're starting so we know what to build from. Your only job is to show up three times and
> leave feeling like you could have done more. That's it. That's the whole assignment."*

This makes leaving reps in the tank *the goal* rather than a compromise, makes attendance the
success criterion, and removes any way for her to fail. You can't fail a calibration.

### Pre-empt this before it happens

**She is going to compare herself to her 17-year-old self.** She was a strong multi-sport
athlete and is about to lift noticeably less than she remembers. For a returning athlete that
comparison is the single most common reason a program dies in week two. Get ahead of it: the
number she starts at is data, not a verdict.

### Three rules — give her these and nothing else

1. **Finish every set thinking you could have done three or four more.** The week 1 failure
   mode isn't undertraining — it's four days of soreness that teaches her the gym feels terrible.
2. **The first session is one set of everything.** Her soreness response is unknown. Finding it
   out costs one session; guessing wrong costs a week.
3. **Success is showing up three times.** Not loads, not reps. Say it in exactly those words.

> ⚠️ **The scale may go up a pound or two** in these first weeks — glycogen and water, not fat.
> Someone chasing weight loss who sees the scale rise in week 2 quits in week 3. Most
> preventable failure in the program, and it costs one sentence.

### What not to worry about yet

- **Progression** — she'll add load fast once calibrated
- **Rest periods and density** — the 45-minute cap is a promise to her, not a target
- **Supersets** — not in this block at all
- **Hitting every rep in the range** — if 8 feels right and the program says 12, she does 8
- **Secondary form faults** — they can wait three weeks

## 8. Week 1 is a selection week

Three choices are provisional. Rather than assume what she can do after a year away, week 1
confirms it — she films a set of each, you watch, the answer picks the movement for the block.

| What she films | What you're looking for | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| Bodyweight squat, then goblet squat | Depth, knee tracking, whether knees fall in under load | Whether slot 1A stays leg press or upgrades to hack / barbell squat |
| Dumbbell RDL, light | Whether she hinges or squats it; whether the back rounds near the bottom | Whether a trap bar deadlift is on the table at all |
| Bodyweight split squat | Balance, pelvic drop, knee valgus on the front leg | Whether slot 1C loads now or stays bodyweight for a couple of weeks |

### Also captured in week 1

- **Working loads at target RIR** on each lift — this *is* the strength baseline, no 1RM testing
- **Movement quality notes** on the three main lifts
- **Frontal-plane screen** — single-leg squat or step-down, watching for pelvic drop and valgus
- **Girths and photos**, since scale weight is explicitly not the KPI
- **Actual session duration** — decides whether a sixth movement goes in later

### Camera angles

| Movement | Angle | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Leg press | 45° front-oblique | Knee tracking, and where her lower back leaves the pad |
| Dumbbell RDL | Straight from the side | Only angle showing spine position and whether hips actually travel back |

### One fault, one cue

The middle column is the whole coaching budget for a working set: one fault, one sentence. The
right column is what sits behind it in the app — she pulls it when she wants it, which is a
different thing from you correcting her on it. **What you actively coach still waits until week
3.** Five corrections on one movement teaches her she's bad at that movement.

| Movement | Fix this first | What to say | Behind the tap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leg press | Knees drifting inward; lower back peeling off the pad | "Feet just wider than your shoulders, knees tracking over your toes." | What the sled actually is, how deep before the hips roll up, whole-foot pressure, no hard lockout |
| DB RDL | Squatting it — knees drift forward, hips never go back | "Push your hips backward, dumbbells sliding down close to your legs." | Knees stay fixed once bent, stretch not depth, why drifting dumbbells load the back |
| **Split squat** | Front knee caving inward, and one hip dropping | "Keep your hips level and your front knee pointing forward." | Back knee travels straight down, weight stays front, and film this one |
| Chest press (flat) | Shoulders shrugging toward the ears | "Pin your shoulder blades down before the first rep." | Seat height, stop short of lockout |
| Incline DB press | Shrugging, and cutting the range short | "Pin your shoulder blades down. Lower the dumbbells to chest level." | Bench angle, kicking the dumbbells into position, no clashing at the top |
| DB overhead press | Ribs flaring, lower back arching | "Keep your back flat — don't lean back to get the weight up." | Shoulder-to-overhead range, why seated keeps her honest, load check |
| Lat pulldown | Leaning back, using momentum | "Pull your elbows down to your ribs without leaning back." | A small lean is fine, full range at the top, grip width |
| Seated cable row | Yanking with the arms and torso | "Keep your torso upright and still — only your arms move." | Lead with the elbows, let the shoulder blades travel, load check |
| Hip abduction machine | Letting the stack drop on the way back in | "Control it on the way back in." | Pads on the knees not the shins, the forward lean, three-second return |
| Standing calf raise | Short, bouncy reps | "Rise up as high as you can, then lower all the way back down." | Heels below the step, knees straight, no bouncing |
| Seated leg curl | Hips lifting off the pad at the end of the curl | "Glue your thighs to the seat and curl underneath you." | Tempo, foot angle |
| DB lateral raise | Traps shrugging, weight swinging up | "Lead with your elbows, shoulders stay down away from your ears." | Exact height — stop at shoulder |
| Easy cardio | Treating the warm-up as a workout | "Conversational pace." | What that actually means, why not running, why five minutes earns its place |

> **Why the cues are worded that way.** Every one points at an *effect in the world* rather than
> a muscle or body part. External cues generally produce better movement and faster learning than
> internal ones, and they're far easier to self-check from video: "squeeze your glute" is
> unverifiable on camera, "hips level" is something she can see herself.
>
> **The second constraint, added later: no word that needs a definition.** The first draft failed
> this in several places — "beltline", "ribs down", "the sled", "toward the wall behind you" — all
> of which are trainer vocabulary she has no reason to own. They're gone from the one-liners and,
> where the idea still mattered, explained in plain words behind the tap. A cue she has to decode
> isn't a cue; it's homework delivered mid-set.

## 10. Autoregulation

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Soccer game the day before** | Drop slot 1 to 2 working sets, cut slot 4 entirely. Keep both upper slots and the abductor work at full volume. Don't rearrange the week. |
| **Only two sessions that week** | Nothing. She does the next two in the queue. Every session is pattern-complete, which is why this failure mode is free. |
| **IT band is talking to her** | Two moves, and they pull apart now that C is both the single-leg day *and* the abduction day. Run **A or B** instead of C to drop the single-leg volume — and put the **abduction machine into slot 5 of whichever session she runs**, displacing the lateral raise or the calf raise for as long as it takes. §5's compounding warning is what makes the second half necessary: abduction no longer appears anywhere except C. Persistent pain gets logged and reported. |
| **Sessions finishing under 35 min** | Signal to add a movement around week 4 — **a chest/press movement** (DB bench, dips, push-up variation). With lateral delt covered by A's raise, chest at 4 direct sets is the thinnest pattern left. Add one, not two. |
| **Lateral knee talks after the leg curl** | Watch item, not an expected one. §11 moderates loaded knee flexion through the irritable range; 2 sets once a week is trivial next to the bike, but it gets logged and reported like any IT band signal. |
| **Topping out the rep range by week 2** | Expected, not a sign it's too easy. Returning athletes regain fast. Keep adding load and let it run. |
| **Form clean for 4–6 weeks** | Trigger for block two: heavier 5–8 range on slot 1, upgrade leg press if week 1 film supported it, *then* consider variety. Technique → intensity → variety. |

## 11. IT band constraints

Etiology is genuinely contested — friction, fat-pad compression and bursal irritation are all
live theories. What *is* well supported is the part that drives programming.

### Emphasize

- **Hip abductor strengthening** — machine abduction, loaded on a stack so it can actually
  progress. **Session C only**, two sets a week, which is the floor and is flagged as such in §5
- **Side planks** train the same lateral chain and are **no longer in the block at all** — they
  were the second casualty of slot 5 losing a movement. §10's lever restores abduction before
  anything else if the band complains
- **Single-leg control**, cued explicitly: knee tracks over the middle toes, no pelvic drop

### Moderate early

- High-rep loaded knee flexion/extension through the irritable range
- Long walking-lunge sets and high-volume step-downs
- Running volume — the classic aggravator
- Stationary bike with the saddle set low, which parks her repeatedly in the sore knee angle

### Skip the foam roller

A randomised controlled trial found no change in IT band stiffness from foam rolling or
stretching. The tissue is collagen-dense and resists deformation. Point soft-tissue work at the
TFL and glutes instead.

**Scope note:** this is programming for an occasional niggle. If it becomes anything that limits
her, that's a referral, not a programming fix.

## 12. Expectations and what to measure

Nutrition isn't being addressed. With three 45-minute sessions and no dietary change, meaningful
*scale* weight loss is unlikely — worth saying in week 1 rather than discovering in week 8.

The good news is specific to her: a detrained former athlete is in the best-case window for
recomposition. Previously trained people regain muscle faster than they originally built it, so
she can add muscle and lose fat concurrently in a way an untrained beginner largely can't.

> **Change the KPI.** Track girths, photos, how clothes fit, and working loads. **Do not let
> scale weight be the measure of success.** The failure mode is obvious once named — she does
> everything right for ten weeks, the scale doesn't move, and she quits.

One optional lever, squarely inside CPT scope: protein around 1.6 g/kg (~0.7 g/lb). General
guidance, not a diet plan.

## 13. Progression timeline

Literature-backed and coaching-estimate items are marked separately.

| Window | What to expect |
|---|---|
| **Weeks 1–2** | Almost entirely neural. Working loads may climb 20–40% — that's her learning to brace and recruit, not tissue. DOMS in week 1, largely gone by week 3. *(estimate)* |
| **Weeks 3–6** | Steep part of the regain curve. Previously trained people show their fastest gains in roughly the first five weeks. Double progression fires nearly every session. First visible changes: posture, shoulders, how clothes sit. |
| **Weeks 6–12** | Strength largely restored — retraining studies put strength back within ~8 weeks and cross-sectional area within ~12. *Caveat: "restored to what" depends on a baseline nobody has measured.* |
| **Months 3–6** | The honest slowdown. Building new tissue rather than reclaiming old; load increases go from weekly to monthly. **Month four is when this program dies if it's going to** — the reward rate collapses right as novelty wears off. That's a coaching intervention, not a programming one. |
| **Months 6–12** | Past regain, into genuine new adaptation. Realistic lean gain for a woman past the beginner phase is roughly 0.25–0.5 lb/month, and that assumes nutrition supports it. *(estimate)* |

**Body composition:** without dietary change, expect visible recomposition around 8–12 weeks and
meaningful change at 4–6 months, with the scale roughly flat throughout. With a protein bump and
a small deficit, pull that forward to ~8–12 weeks.

**Then discount all of it.** Three sessions a week is nominal; with her schedule she'll
realistically average two to two-and-a-half. Adherence is the dominant variable in every number
above, and it outweighs any programming decision in this document.

## 14. Design rationale

**Confidence scale:** ● Well supported — meta-analyses or replicated outcome studies point this
way. ◐ Mixed — real but contested, indirect, or doesn't settle the question. ○ Coaching
judgment — defensible, but no study made this call.

### ● 01 — Why 8–12 reps, not heavier

Schoenfeld's meta-analysis of 21 studies comparing low load (≤60% 1RM) to high load found
**hypertrophy similar between conditions** when sets went to failure, while **1RM strength
favored heavier loads**. That split is the argument: 8–12 buys essentially all of the muscle and
most of the strength without asking someone re-learning technique to grind near-maximal weights.

It also settles *when* to go heavier. Because strength — not size — is the outcome that cares
about load, the heavier 5–8 work belongs in block two, once form is confirmed.

*Her version: "Heavy enough to matter, light enough that your form doesn't fall apart."*

### ● 02 — Why only two sets of most things

Not the compromise it looks like. A meta-analysis comparing volume categories found
**significant differences between 1 set and 2–3 sets, but no significant difference between 2–3
and 4–6 sets.** The jump from one hard set to two or three is where most of the return lives.

The ACSM minimum-effective-dose work puts the hypertrophy floor at roughly **4 sets per muscle
group per week** — which this program clears on every pattern. A 2025 trial had 42 trained men
and women perform a *single* set of nine exercises twice weekly for eight weeks, with both
to-failure and submaximal groups showing appreciable gains.

Layered on: the recent meta-regression found **strength shows steeper diminishing returns than
hypertrophy**, and her goal leads with strength. Indirect "fractional" sets also count, so a
compound-based full-body program delivers more per muscle than the nominal count suggests.

> **Keeping this honest:** "no significant difference" in a categorical comparison isn't
> equivalence — it's partly a statement about statistical power. Newer meta-regressions pooling
> more studies *do* detect a continuing slope of ~0.37% additional gain per set. Both are true:
> the marginal set keeps adding a little, and the amount is small enough that categorical
> analyses often can't see it.

**Practical consequence:** this weakens the case for reflexively adding a sixth movement at week
4. If 2–3 hard sets already captures most of the available adaptation, the reason to add
something is that she has time and wants to — not a volume deficit. Pulling at six direct sets a
week clears the floor comfortably; the one genuine thin spot is **chest at four**, sitting exactly
on it. (Lateral delt was thinner still at two — under the floor — which A5's raise fixes.)

*Her version: "Two hard sets gets you most of what four would. The rest is time you don't have to spend."*

### ● 03 — Why 2–3 RIR, tightening to 1–2

The proximity-to-failure meta-regressions found **strength gains similar across a wide range of
RIR, while hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure.**

So the conservative start costs almost nothing on the goal she leads with. Leaving three or four
reps in the tank during calibration is close to free in strength terms — which means the
adherence argument wins outright, because there's nothing real on the other side of the scale.

*Her version: "Finish every set feeling like you had a few more. Early on that costs you nothing."*

### ◐ 04 — Why full-body three times a week

**For hypertrophy, frequency doesn't meaningfully matter when volume is equated.** Training a
muscle once a week produces similar growth to twice or three times. **For strength, higher
frequencies may be modestly better, with diminishing returns.** Since her goal leads with
strength, the second half is the relevant half.

> **Why the popular mechanism doesn't carry this.** The usual argument is that muscle protein
> synthesis stays elevated 24–48 h, so you train each muscle every other day to keep "coverage"
> high. Reasonable model, doesn't survive the outcome data. Damas et al. showed **early-training
> MPS elevations are largely directed at repairing muscle damage, not accreting new tissue** —
> MPS only begins tracking hypertrophy after the damage response attenuates, around week three.
> A separate study found acute post-exercise myofibrillar protein synthesis was *not correlated*
> with eventual hypertrophy in young men at all. The window is real as a measurement, but much of
> what it registers early is repair. That's why volume-equated frequency trials return null.

What justifies three full-body sessions:

- **It distributes volume**, keeping each session inside 45 minutes
- **Every session is pattern-complete**, so her schedule stops being a programming problem
- **Three exposures is three rehearsals.** Strength is substantially a skill — the most likely
  explanation for the frequency-favors-strength finding, and the same argument as keeping to
  a small movement set, reached from the other direction

**Transferable lesson:** when a mechanistic model and outcome trials disagree, trust the trials.
MPS is measured in hours, hypertrophy accrues over months, and the bridge is weaker than the
mechanism implies. The foam-rolling story is the same shape.

### ● 05 — Why one protected lift with full rest

Rest-interval meta-analyses found **longer rest modestly favors strength** (SMD ≈ −0.74) while
**hypertrophy differences are trivial** (SMD ≈ 0.08). That asymmetry says exactly where to spend
a limited time budget: full rest on the one lift where it measurably changes the outcome,
density everywhere else.

*Her version: "Take your time on the first lift. Move briskly through the rest."*

### ○ 06 — Why rotate squat, hinge and single-leg

No study picks these three. The reasoning is structural: no single lower-body exercise covers the
lower body, and these three divide it about as cleanly as anything can.

The unilateral slot earns its place for reasons specific to her. It **exposes side-to-side
asymmetry** that bilateral work lets the strong side hide — likely relevant given a one-sided IT
band history. It's the movement where knee tracking and pelvic drop become *visible on video*,
which matters for remote coaching. And sprinting, cutting and decelerating in soccer are all
single-leg.

**Hold this loosely:** unilateral work has a high early return and a low ceiling, because you
can't progressively load something whose limiter is balance. Block-one choice, not a permanent
one. Related: session C's slot 1 is a control day wearing a strength day's clothes — defensible
early, but the label is misleading.

### ● 07 — Why hip abductor work in (almost) every session

Highest-confidence claim in the program, and the reason slot 5 exists at all.

Fredericson's study of 24 distance runners with ITBS put them through a six-week protocol
emphasizing gluteus medius. **Female athletes gained an average 34.9% hip abductor torque in the
injured limb. 22 of 24 were pain-free and back to running at six weeks, with no recurrences at
six months.** The broader clinical literature agrees abductor weakness is characteristic of ITBS
and strengthening improves symptoms.

**Caveats:** small and uncontrolled, and the mechanism of ITBS is still contested. But the
*intervention* has held up even as the mechanism hasn't been settled.

> **What the delivered program actually runs, and what that trades.** This section argues for
> abduction in every session. The program runs it in **one** — session C, two sets a week (§3) —
> and no longer pairs it with side-plank work on the same lateral chain, because that is gone too.
>
> An earlier draft of this note argued the reduction cost little: Fredericson's protocol can't
> separate dose from outcome, so nothing in the evidence base distinguishes four loaded sets a
> week from six. That argument stretches to four. It does **not** stretch to two with no
> lateral-chain support, and two is below the ~4 sets/muscle/week floor §14 ●02 applies
> everywhere else in this document. Saying otherwise would be the document talking itself into
> something.
>
> What actually justifies it is adherence, not physiology. Slot 5 used to live inside slots 2
> and 3's rest periods and wasn't getting done, and work that doesn't happen has no dose at all.
> That is a real argument. It is also a different argument from *this is enough*, and the two
> shouldn't be allowed to blur.
>
> So this is the **one** place where a reduction gets reversed on symptom rather than on
> schedule. If the IT band starts talking, abduction goes into slot 5 on all three days before
> anything else in the program changes (§10).

*Her version: "This is the thing that keeps your IT band quiet. It's why we don't skip it."*

### ◐ 08 — Why the abduction machine over band walks

Two reasons of unequal strength.

**The solid one is progressive overload.** If the mechanism behind 07 is *strength*, the exercise
has to be loadable. A band has maybe four discrete tensions and can't be logged. A weight stack can.

**The weaker, more interesting one:** EMG work found the abduction machine gives the best
**gluteus medius–to–TFL ratio** of the common options. That matters here specifically because
*the TFL is the muscle that tensions the IT band* — recruiting a lot of it while trying to fix an
IT band problem works against you.

**Why "mixed":** EMG measures acute activation, not adaptation, and is routinely overstated.
Findings also aren't unanimous — side-lying abduction shows the highest raw glute med activation,
and at least one study had elastic resistance beating the seated machine. Treat the ratio as a
tiebreaker, not a proof.

**Where this left the band:** nowhere. The warm-up band walk was the last thing in the program
the argument above didn't apply to, and it came out (§5) — if a band can't be loaded or logged,
it can't be the thing doing the work, and it was costing two minutes of a 45-minute cap to be a
worse version of slot 5.

*Her version: "The machine lets us actually add weight over time — that's the whole reason it beats a band."*

### ○ 09 — Repeat where skill is the limiter, vary where it isn't

Rotating exercises costs reps per movement — but only the skill-demanding ones care. The three
lower lifts recur because technique is their limiter and frequency is
how technique is acquired. The upper-body and accessory slots vary because a machine press or a
cable row has almost no skill floor, so variety there is nearly free — and it's what keeps three
sessions from collapsing into one session on repeat, which is an adherence cost with no
offsetting benefit. Motor learning principles support the repetition half; the variety half is
an adherence judgment. Total: 12 movements, but never more than 5 exercises in a session, and
the hard ones are always the familiar ones.

*Her version: "The hard lifts stay the same so you get good at them. The rest changes so you don't get bored."*

## 15. How to present this

**Two audiences, and only one of them wants this document.** Everything above is for you. Almost
none of it should reach her up front — her limiter is adherence, and a rationale document is
cognitive load applied to someone who mainly needs to know what to do on Tuesday. Handing a
returning athlete an evidence dossier signals the program is complicated, which is the opposite
of what you want her believing in week one.

**What she gets up front:** the five slots in order, one sentence on why the abductor work
matters, the three calibration rules. Nothing else.

**The app applies the same rule per movement.** One cue on screen during a set — never two — with
the fuller explanation, and a demo video, one tap away for when she wants it. Same principle as
this document: the depth exists, it just doesn't arrive uninvited.

**When to deploy the rest:**
- **When she asks.** Curiosity is buy-in; answer it properly.
- **When adherence dips.** Understanding why a thing works is a real motivator at week six.
- **When someone at the gym contradicts you.** This *will* happen in a campus rec center, and
  "my brother has a study" beats "my brother said so."

**The framing that does the most work:** lead with what the program *doesn't* ask of her. Three
sessions, five exercises a session, 40 minutes, no supersets, nothing she doesn't already recognize. The
persuasive thing about this program isn't how well-evidenced it is — it's how little it demands.

## 16. The three weakest links

If this program underdelivers, these are the likeliest reasons, and none are things the evidence
base can fix.

1. **Nutrition isn't being addressed.** Her stated goal includes weight loss and the program
   cannot deliver much of that alone. Every number here is training adaptation, not body composition.
2. **Adherence is the dominant variable and it isn't in the program.** Three sessions is nominal;
   two to two-and-a-half is realistic. That outweighs every design decision here combined.
3. **Her actual starting point is unknown.** "Strength restored in eight weeks" means restored to
   a baseline nobody has measured. Week one's film and loads are the first real data.
